Verification Begins with the Claim

Learning objectives: Check claims and transformations against appropriate evidence, detect uncertainty or bias, and decide whether to accept, revise, verify further, or reject an output.; Match important claims to suitable evidence and record the check.

Content

Verification begins with the claim

Generative AI can produce useful summaries, explanations, and drafts. It can also state an incorrect detail in fluent language, combine separate facts, or present a guess as though it came from a source. Verification is the work of checking a specific claim against evidence that is suitable for that claim. It is not a general feeling that a paragraph “looks right,” and it is not asking the same tool to reassure you.

This reading presents a claim-by-claim method. It explains how to select evidence, distinguish primary source material from generated explanations, check numbers, and record the next action. Examples use fictional workplace information only.

Figure: Match each claim to suitable evidence and keep uncertainty visible.

Separate a draft into claims

A paragraph often contains more factual claims than it first appears to. “The north entrance will close Friday because a maintenance inspection found a fault, and policy requires 48 hours’ notice” contains at least three claims: the entrance will close Friday; a maintenance inspection found a fault; and a policy requires 48 hours’ notice. A reader may accept the sentence as a whole because the explanation sounds coherent. A reviewer should separate its parts and check them individually.

A claim is a statement that a reader might treat as true: a date, name, quantity, policy, cause, quotation, status, or description of a person or group. Recommendations may also rely on factual assumptions. Ask what facts would need to be true for the recommendation to make sense.

Match each claim to suitable evidence

Different claims need different sources. A current approved schedule can confirm a time. The current policy can establish a rule. An underlying record can support a count. A person with direct responsibility may confirm a change that has not yet been documented. The key is not simply to locate a source, but to find one with authority over the detail.

A broad web search is not automatically the right evidence for an internal procedure. A previous-year policy may not support a current rule. A colleague’s recollection may be useful but could need confirmation. If a claim could affect safety, money, access, or rights, use the appropriate formal source or responsible reviewer.

Check the exact passage or record that supports the wording. A summary saying “The schedule confirms Friday” is generated explanation; the schedule itself is the evidence. A citation, hyperlink, or page title can be a useful lead, but it must be opened and examined. Does it refer to the correct organization and date? Does the passage support the precise claim? Is there a qualification nearby? A source that discusses a related topic does not necessarily support the sentence you want to publish.

Use a claim log

A claim log keeps review focused. For each important claim, record:

  • The exact claim or a short label.
  • The source that can answer it.
  • The result of checking that source.
  • The action you took.

For instance: “Entrance closes Friday — current facilities schedule — matched — retain.” “Inspection found a fault — report not supplied — unverified — remove cause and ask facilities.” “48-hour notice is required — current policy needed — not checked — do not state as policy.” This structure makes clear what was verified and what remains open.

Use actions such as retain, correct, qualify, verify further, remove, or escalate. “Not verified” is an acceptable result. It does not mean the claim is necessarily false; it means there is not enough evidence to present it as verified. If the fact is necessary, find the responsible source or person. If it is not necessary, remove it.

Check numbers and categories

Numbers can look objective while still being misleading. Verify the underlying entries, the calculation, and the label attached to the result. Suppose a fictional table shows 18 text messages delivered and 20 visitors affected by a schedule change. Before writing “18 of 20 affected visitors received a text,” confirm that both counts use the same time period, the same visitor group, and a consistent definition of “delivered.” A calculation may be correct while its categories do not match.

If you compute a percentage, recalculate it or use a trusted calculator, then check that the denominator answers the question. If the data counts visits rather than people, do not describe the count as unique participants. If entries are missing, state that limitation. Precision in a number does not compensate for unclear definitions.

Scale the review to the stakes

Not every sentence needs the same review effort. A low-stakes brainstorming idea may need a quick check. A deadline, safety instruction, eligibility rule, pay amount, or claim about a person warrants stronger evidence and often another qualified reviewer. The higher the potential cost of an error, the more carefully you should verify and the less comfortable you should be with unresolved uncertainty.

When evidence is unavailable, do not let fluent wording fill the gap. Mark the claim as unresolved, ask the source owner, or remove it. If an unsupported statement could lead to action affecting someone, pause and use the appropriate review process. A disclosure that AI helped does not make the claim reliable.

A short verification routine

  1. Underline factual claims and assumptions in the output.
  2. Label the claim type: date, procedure, count, cause, quotation, status, or recommendation.
  3. Identify the source with authority over that detail.
  4. Inspect the relevant source passage or record.
  5. Record whether it matches, conflicts, or remains unresolved.
  6. Correct, qualify, retain, remove, or escalate as appropriate.
  7. Review the changed draft for new unsupported claims.

For example, a fictional schedule update might say: “The east entrance closes at 4 p.m. Thursday because inspectors found a structural problem.” Check the closure time against the current building notice. Check the cause against the inspection report or facilities contact. If the notice confirms the time but no cause is available, retain the time and remove the explanation. Do not imply that the whole building is closed unless the source says so.

Keep evidence and explanation distinct

A conversational tool can help organize claims, suggest questions, or point to a section of supplied text. Those functions can make review faster, but they do not transfer responsibility. Confirm key details in the underlying source, especially when the stakes are high. The source material, your checking notes, and the generated summary are different things; keep them distinguishable.

The practical outcome is not a perfect-looking paragraph. It is a draft whose important claims have appropriate support, whose limits are visible, and whose reviewer can explain what was checked. That is the basis for responsible decisions about whether to accept, revise, verify further, or reject the output.
