Match the Check to the Claim

Learning objectives: Check claims and transformations against appropriate evidence, detect uncertainty or bias, and decide whether to accept, revise, verify further, or reject an output.; Match important claims to suitable evidence and record the check.

Content

Lecture 1: Match the check to the claim

Duration: approximately 10 minutes (script targets a measured, reflective delivery) Objectives: verify-ai-claims Purpose: Teach learners to identify important claims and choose evidence that can actually support or correct them.

Verification starts by separating a draft into claims. This lecture uses a fictional staff update with a date, a procedure, and a numerical result. Learners match each claim to a suitable source: an approved schedule for dates, a current policy for procedures, and the underlying record or calculation for numbers. They distinguish source evidence from an AI-generated explanation and practice recording what was checked, what was uncertain, and what action followed. The key rule is that verification effort should fit the claim’s stakes and the evidence available; confidence or a plausible citation is not enough.

Lecture script

Lecture 1 script: Match the check to the claim

Objectives: verify-ai-claims Target: approximately 10 minutes at a measured pace, with time to inspect the sample claim log.

A generated draft may contain three sentences that look equally confident. They may need very different checks. A date should be checked against a schedule. A procedure should be checked against the current policy. A number should be checked against the original record or calculation. Verification is not a ritual of asking the AI whether it is correct. It is a deliberate match between a claim and evidence that can support or correct it.

This lecture extends our recurring principle: know the input, own the output. Last week, we practiced improving a draft and preserving the writer’s voice. This week we focus on factual reliability, uncertainty, and bias. The first move is to slow down and identify what the output actually asserts.

Here is a fictional scenario. A coordinator at the made-up Harbor Street Community Center needs a short staff update about a room schedule. A generated draft says: “Room B will close on Wednesday, June 10, because the annual inspection found an electrical fault. The center’s approved procedure requires all room closures to be posted 48 hours in advance. Last month, 18 of 20 visitors affected by a closure received a text.” The prompt only provided a sample room schedule and a short fictional attendance table. No inspection report, closure policy, notice requirement, or text-message record was supplied.

The draft’s claims can be separated into units. Claim one: Room B will close on June 10. Claim two: an inspection found an electrical fault. Claim three: policy requires 48-hour notice. Claim four: 18 of 20 visitors received a text last month. Each requires a different check. If you treat the entire paragraph as one block, you may overlook a claim hidden inside a plausible explanation.

For the date and room, consult the authoritative schedule or a person authorized to maintain it. For a safety-related cause, use the actual inspection report or confirmation from the responsible facilities contact. For a policy claim, locate the current policy version and check its exact wording. For the notification count, inspect the underlying communication log and confirm how “affected visitor” and “received a text” were defined. A model-generated summary of a source may help you find a relevant passage, but the summary itself is not the source.

This distinction matters: evidence is the underlying material that supports a claim; generated explanation is wording about that material. If a tool says, “The policy requires 48 hours,” ask to see or locate the policy passage and confirm that it applies to this room and this type of closure. A citation-shaped sentence is not proof. A link might be outdated, irrelevant, or misrepresented. Open the actual source when possible and confirm that the specific detail appears there.

Sometimes a direct source is unavailable. Then represent uncertainty honestly. You might mark the claim “not verified,” ask the responsible person, or remove the claim from the message. Do not turn an absence of evidence into evidence that the claim is false—or into a reason to accept it. Keep the audience informed about what is known and what remains open.

A useful claim log has four columns: claim, best available evidence, check result, and action. For our example, it might say: “Room B closes June 10 — current room schedule — matched — retain.” “Inspection found electrical fault — inspection report not provided — unverified — remove cause and ask facilities.” “48-hour notice — current closure procedure needed — unverified — do not state as policy.” “18 of 20 received a text — fictional tracking table unavailable — not checked — omit number.” This log makes a person’s reasoning visible and separates facts from generated language.

How much checking is enough? Match the effort to both the stakes and the type of claim. A typo in a casual brainstorming note may need a quick correction. A deadline, safety instruction, eligibility rule, pay amount, or statement about a person deserves a more careful check with an authoritative source. When the cost of being wrong is high, use more than one appropriate verification step or ask a qualified person to review. When there is no reliable evidence, escalate or leave the claim out rather than manufacture certainty.

You can use a quick sequence. One: underline each claim that a reader could reasonably treat as fact. Two: tag the claim type—date, policy, number, attribution, causal explanation, or recommendation. Three: name a source that can answer that exact question. Four: inspect the relevant part of the source, not just a summary. Five: record the result and the action: retain, correct, qualify, verify further, or remove. Six: confirm that edits did not introduce new unsupported details.

The word “source” can mean different things. A current official schedule may be appropriate for a room time. A policy document may be the right source for procedure. A person with direct responsibility may clarify an unrecorded change, though it may be useful to document the confirmation. A general web search is not automatically authoritative just because it returns many results. Choose the source that has authority over the claim.

Checking numbers deserves special attention. Confirm the source data, the calculation, and the labels. If a table says 18 visitors received a text and 20 were affected, “18 of 20” may be accurate only if both counts cover the same dates and the same group. A calculation can be arithmetically correct and still misleading if the categories do not match. If the output computes a percentage, recompute it or use a trusted calculator, then make sure the denominator is appropriate. Do not let a precise-looking number hide a definition problem.

Claims about people or groups need care as well. A generated draft may describe a group as “unresponsive,” “difficult,” or “less engaged” based on incomplete examples. Ask what evidence supports the label and whether the wording treats people fairly. If it is a summary of feedback, compare the description with the actual comments and look for perspectives that were left out. We will examine bias and uncertainty more directly in the next lecture and reading.

What if the AI provides a source? Treat that as a lead to check. Open it, confirm that it is the intended source, locate the passage, and see whether the passage supports the exact wording. If the service cannot provide a usable source, ask for the claim to be labeled as unverified or remove it. Do not rely on a made-up title, a dead link, or a citation that merely resembles a real reference.

Let’s recap. Break a draft into individual claims. Match each claim to evidence with authority over that subject. Inspect the evidence yourself when feasible. Keep source material separate from generated explanation. Record what you checked and what you did next. If you cannot verify a high-stakes detail, pause, ask, or remove it. Next, we’ll use this evidence-first approach to recognize uncertainty and bias, and to decide when a claim should be corrected or rejected.
