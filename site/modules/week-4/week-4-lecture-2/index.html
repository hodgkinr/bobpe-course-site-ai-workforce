Read Uncertainty and Correct the Record

Learning objectives: Check claims and transformations against appropriate evidence, detect uncertainty or bias, and decide whether to accept, revise, verify further, or reject an output.; Explain when AI is useful, when another method is preferable, and how to communicate the role and limits of AI-assisted work.; Recognize missing information, uncertainty, and potential bias in an output.; Correct, qualify, escalate, or reject claims that the evidence does not support.

Content

Lecture 2: Read uncertainty and correct the record

Duration: approximately 10 minutes (script targets a measured, reflective delivery) Objectives: assess-uncertainty, correct-unsupported-claims Purpose: Help learners recognize limits in evidence and language, notice possible bias, and take a proportionate next step.

This lecture shows how uncertainty enters a draft through missing sources, ambiguous categories, conflicting records, and overconfident wording. In a fictional program summary, a generated sentence claims that one group of visitors is “less committed” based on incomplete attendance figures. Learners distinguish what the data shows from what it cannot explain, check for omitted context and unfair labels, and revise the sentence to describe observed evidence without inferring motives. A decision ladder—accept, qualify, verify further, correct, or reject—supports action. Learners finish by documenting the change and escalating when the claim could affect safety, rights, or access.

Lecture script

Lecture 2 script: Read uncertainty and correct the record

Objectives: assess-uncertainty, correct-unsupported-claims Target: approximately 10 minutes at a measured pace, including reflection on the sample summary.

A statement can be wrong because the evidence is weak. It can also be misleading because it sounds more certain than the evidence allows, leaves out an important group, or uses a label that turns a limited observation into a judgment about people. Responsible review includes checking facts and noticing how the output frames them.

Let’s use a fictional example from the made-up Harbor Street Community Center. A coordinator provides an AI tool with a small attendance table. The table covers four optional evening workshops in one month. It records 12 visits from Group A and 6 visits from Group B. It does not show how many people in each group were invited, whether everyone had transportation, whether the sessions were accessible, or whether a person might attend more than once. The generated summary says: “Group B is less committed to learning and should receive fewer workshop invitations.”

What does the table support? It supports a count of recorded visits during those four sessions, assuming the data were entered correctly. It does not tell us how many unique people attended, how many people were invited, why someone did or did not attend, or whether the groups had equal access. It certainly does not establish that one group is less committed. And the recommendation to send fewer invitations is not a direct consequence of the count. That sentence moves from observation to interpretation to action without evidence for the steps in between.

This is an example of overclaiming. The wording turns a limited measure into a broad conclusion. A more careful summary might say: “The table records 12 visits from Group A and 6 visits from Group B across four evening workshops. The table does not show invitation totals, unique participants, or reasons for attendance, so it cannot explain the difference.” That sentence is still useful: it reports what is visible and names what is missing.

Uncertainty can come from several places. The information may be incomplete: a relevant schedule, policy, or record was not included. The source may be stale: a policy changed after the copy was made. The categories may be ambiguous: “visit” may count repeat participation. The tool may have summarized unevenly or misread a table. The language may be too strong for the evidence. A fair review asks not only “Is this sentence true?” but also “What exactly does the evidence let us say?”

Uncertainty is not failure. It is useful information for deciding the next step. A sentence such as “The current table does not identify unique visitors” tells a reviewer what remains unknown. A qualified statement might say “In this small sample…” or “The available records show…” if those phrases accurately describe the evidence. But qualifiers are not a license to keep an unsupported inference. “Possibly less committed” still makes a claim about motivation without evidence.

Bias can enter through data selection, categories, missing perspectives, or wording. A sample may overrepresent people who had easier access. A comparison may treat different circumstances as if they were equal. A description may label a group by a behavior that the data do not measure. The reviewer should ask: Who is included? Who is missing? Are the groups or time periods comparable? What assumptions are built into the category? Does the language describe an observed action or judge a person’s character? Could the conclusion affect access, opportunity, or treatment?

These are not questions that a tool can settle by itself. A generated explanation may help point out a possible issue, but it does not guarantee that the issue was found or resolved. Ask people familiar with the context when their perspective is needed. In consequential situations, use established review channels and qualified decision makers. Avoid asking a model to make decisions about a person’s eligibility, performance, or worth based on a thin summary.

A practical decision ladder helps convert review into action:

  • Accept when the claim matches reliable evidence and is appropriate for the task.
  • Qualify when the evidence supports a narrower statement but not a broad one.
  • Verify further when a suitable source may exist but has not yet been checked.
  • Correct when the right evidence is available and the draft states it incorrectly.
  • Reject or remove when a claim is unsupported, irrelevant, or cannot responsibly be established.

For our attendance example, the numeric count might be retained after checking the source table. The statement about commitment should be rejected. The recommendation to reduce invitations should be removed. If the center needs to understand access, staff could ask a better question and collect appropriate evidence with safeguards—for instance, whether participants encountered scheduling or transportation barriers. That is a new inquiry, not a conclusion the existing table supports.

Correction should be specific and traceable. Record the original claim, the evidence reviewed, the change, and any remaining question. A correction note could say: “Removed ‘less committed’ and the recommendation to send fewer invitations. The table records visits, not motivation or invitation totals. Retained the counts after checking the supplied table. Follow-up needed: determine whether invitations and access were comparable.” This helps a colleague see both the correction and its limits.

Sometimes the appropriate next step is escalation. If an unsupported statement could affect safety, access, rights, money, or someone’s employment, do not quietly rewrite it and move on if the underlying issue needs an authorized decision. Pause publication or action, preserve the relevant record, and ask the responsible person or process to review. If the claim is low-stakes and irrelevant, removing it may be enough. The response should match the potential impact.

Be careful with certainty words. “Proves,” “always,” “never,” “everyone,” and “caused” carry strong meanings. A small sample may show a pattern in that sample, but not prove what happens in every setting. A timing relationship does not by itself establish cause. A statement may be true for one period but stale now. Choose wording that reflects the source and the scope.

Here is a short practice. Read a fictional report sentence: “Because fewer evening attendees came from Group B, they are not interested in future training.” Underline the observed measure. Circle the inference. List one missing piece of information. Rewrite it to report only what is supported and explain the uncertainty. A possible revision is: “The attendance sheet records fewer visits from Group B in the four evening sessions. It does not record invitation totals or reasons for attendance, so it cannot establish interest.” Then identify what a responsible next check might be.

If no AI tool is available, this review still applies to a report written by a person or a spreadsheet summary. If an AI service helped create the report, it may be useful to note its role, but the central task remains checking evidence and correcting the claim.

Recap: uncertainty can come from missing, stale, ambiguous, or uneven evidence. Distinguish what the source shows from what the draft infers. Look for missing perspectives and labels that judge people beyond the evidence. Use accept, qualify, verify further, correct, or reject as explicit choices. Keep a correction record, and escalate when the stakes require an authorized human decision. The activity this week asks you to audit a flawed workplace draft and produce that correction record.
