Steer the Draft Through Iteration

Learning objectives: Turn a workplace need into a clear request with goal, audience, context, constraints, and a useful output format; then improve the request through iteration.; Use targeted follow-up directions to improve a draft against explicit criteria.

Content

Lecture 1: Steer the draft through iteration

Duration: approximately 10 minutes (script targets a measured, reflective delivery) Objectives: iterate-ai-drafts Purpose: Show how follow-up directions can diagnose and improve an AI-generated draft while keeping the human responsible for the goal and final choices.

A first response is a starting point, not a verdict. This lecture models a two-round process: identify what is useful and what is missing, then give a specific follow-up tied to audience, purpose, constraints, and evidence. A fictional community-center coordinator needs a short reminder about a schedule change. Learners compare vague requests (“make it better”) with observable directions (“put the date first, use a calm tone, keep it under 90 words, and do not invent a reason”). They practice separating a writing problem from a factual problem: style can be revised, but facts require an approved source. The lecture closes with a human decision log that records what was accepted, changed, checked, and omitted.

Lecture script

Lecture 1 script: Steer the draft through iteration

Objectives: iterate-ai-drafts Target: approximately 10 minutes at a measured 120–140 words per minute; pause for the worked example and learner reflection.

Imagine you are helping a fictional community center tell visitors that its front desk will open later next Tuesday. You have a goal, a small set of approved facts, and a reader who needs to know what to do. A conversational AI service can help shape a draft. But the first response is not the finished message. It is a candidate that you inspect, steer, and decide whether to use.

This week is about iteration: making a task better through rounds of direction and review. You already learned to frame a request with purpose, audience, context, constraints, and format. Iteration builds on that foundation. Instead of throwing away your task brief when the first answer disappoints, you use it to diagnose the gap and direct a useful next step.

Here is our fictional task. The center’s posted notice says: “On Tuesday, May 12, the front desk will open at 10:00 a.m. instead of 9:00 a.m. The building itself will open at 8:30 a.m. Program times are unchanged.” The coordinator wants a short notice for visitors. No reason for the schedule change has been supplied. That missing reason matters: a draft must not invent one.

Suppose the first draft says: “The center will open late next Tuesday because of staff training. All activities begin at 10 a.m. We apologize for the inconvenience.” It sounds smooth, but fluency can distract us from defects. “Staff training” is unsupported. “The center will open late” could imply the building is closed until 10. And “all activities begin at 10” contradicts the supplied note that program times do not change. Before asking for another draft, identify the problem in concrete terms.

A useful diagnosis has three parts. First, name what is already working. Perhaps the draft is brief and recognizes that visitors need a clear schedule. Second, name the specific defects: invented cause, ambiguous building access, and false program-time change. Third, say what the reader must understand after reading. The center opens at 8:30; the front desk opens at 10:00; programs keep their posted times. This diagnosis makes the next instruction more useful than “try again.”

A follow-up could say: “Revise this for visitors in a calm, direct tone. Put Tuesday, May 12, and the 10:00 a.m. front-desk opening near the beginning. State that the building opens at 8:30 a.m. and program times are unchanged. Keep it under 90 words. Do not add a reason or other facts. If any detail is unclear, list the question instead of guessing.” These directions are observable: you can check the date, the two opening times, the unchanged programs, the length, and whether unsupported material appears.

Notice what the follow-up does not do. It does not ask the AI to decide what the center’s policy should be. It does not provide private information. It does not claim that the model can determine the true schedule. The supplied notice remains the source of the facts. The model can rearrange and phrase those facts, but a human checks the result against the source.

A possible second draft might read: “On Tuesday, May 12, the front desk will open at 10:00 a.m. The building will be open from 8:30 a.m., and scheduled programs will begin at their usual times. Thank you for planning ahead.” That looks more accurate. Still, we review it. Does “the building will be open from 8:30” match the source? Yes. Does “usual times” communicate that programs are unchanged? Likely, though “program times are unchanged” may be clearer. Does it stay under 90 words? Yes. Is the tone appropriate? Probably. The coordinator can choose to retain the clearer wording and remove anything unnecessary.

This is the central pattern: set a goal, inspect, diagnose, direct, inspect again, and decide. There is no magic number of rounds. A second revision might fix a particular issue, or it might introduce a new one. More rounds are not automatically better. Set a stopping condition before you begin: for example, stop when the draft meets the stated criteria and all factual details match the source, or stop after two revisions and finish manually. If a critical fact is missing, pause and ask the source owner rather than prompting the model to fill the gap.

It also helps to distinguish different kinds of feedback. A style issue is about how something sounds: too formal, too long, or too technical for the audience. A structure issue is about order: perhaps the date or action is buried. A factual issue is about whether a statement matches evidence. A scope issue is about the response adding claims or tasks you did not request. A useful follow-up names the category and gives a concrete correction. “Move the opening time into the first sentence” is clearer than “make it flow.” “Remove the reason because it is not in the notice” is clearer than “be more accurate.”

You may also ask for options when the choice is genuinely yours. For example: “Give me two versions, one warm and one very concise. Keep the facts identical.” Then compare the alternatives against the audience and purpose. More options are helpful only if they illuminate a real choice. If you already know the message must be plain and brief, asking for ten tones may add work without improving the result.

How do you know whether the process is helping? Look for evidence tied to your task. Did the revision remove the invented reason? Did it separate building access from front-desk service? Is the program information correct? Can the intended reader find the time quickly? A response that sounds more polished but fails these checks is not an improvement. Quality is measured against the need, not the model’s confidence or the number of changes.

Keep a short decision log as you work. It can be as simple as four notes: accepted, changed, checked, and omitted. “Accepted: date is prominent. Changed: replaced ‘usual times’ with ‘program times are unchanged.’ Checked: all times against the posted notice. Omitted: an invented explanation.” This record makes your role visible and helps another person understand how the draft was produced. It is also useful when you later decide whether disclosure is appropriate under a workplace policy or the expectations of your audience.

For your own practice, choose a low-risk fictional message. Start with a short task brief and a small set of facts. Ask for a draft. Mark one strength and two specific problems. Then give a follow-up that addresses those problems without changing the facts or goal. Compare the next version with your criteria. If no AI service is available, use the same steps on a draft written by a partner, or revise the text yourself while pretending the first draft came from a tool. The skill is steering and judgment, not access to a particular product.

Let’s recap. A first response is a draft. Diagnose the exact gap instead of saying “make it better.” Give observable follow-up directions. Keep the source of facts separate from generated wording. Check whether the revision actually meets the criteria, and stop when it does—or pause when evidence is missing. Record what you accepted, changed, checked, and omitted. In the next lecture, we will focus on a closely related question: how to adapt a draft while preserving your own voice and communicating the role AI played.
