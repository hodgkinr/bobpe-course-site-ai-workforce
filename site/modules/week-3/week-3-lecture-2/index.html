Preserve Voice and Communicate Assistance

Learning objectives: Turn a workplace need into a clear request with goal, audience, context, constraints, and a useful output format; then improve the request through iteration.; Explain when AI is useful, when another method is preferable, and how to communicate the role and limits of AI-assisted work.; Preserve accurate meaning, audience fit, and the writer’s intended voice.; Make an appropriate decision about communicating AI assistance.

Content

Lecture 2: Preserve voice and communicate assistance

Duration: approximately 10 minutes (script targets a measured, reflective delivery) Objectives: preserve-human-voice, disclose-ai-assistance Purpose: Help learners use AI to support communication without surrendering authorship, audience awareness, or appropriate transparency.

This lecture treats voice as a set of choices—wording, emphasis, warmth, and level of detail—rather than a decorative style. In a fictional job-seeker scenario, an AI-generated follow-up email sounds polished but promises more than the learner can deliver. Learners learn to identify what sounds unlike them, what is inaccurate, and what must be retained for the reader. The lecture distinguishes editing assistance from authorship and explains that disclosure depends on policy, stakes, audience expectations, and the nature of the assistance. A four-question disclosure check helps learners decide whether to tell a recipient or supervisor that AI helped, while avoiding both unnecessary disclosure theater and concealment of meaningful reliance.

Lecture script

Lecture 2 script: Preserve voice and communicate assistance

Objectives: preserve-human-voice, disclose-ai-assistance Target: approximately 10 minutes at a measured pace; pause during the revision exercise.

Think about the last time you received a message that sounded polished but did not sound like the person who sent it. Perhaps it used words they never use, made a promise they could not keep, or buried the simple point under formal language. When AI helps write, a similar mismatch can happen. The language may be smooth, while the message no longer reflects the writer’s intent or the relationship with the reader.

Today we will focus on two responsibilities: preserving a human voice and communicating AI assistance when that information matters. “Voice” does not mean keeping every sentence exactly as you first wrote it. It means making deliberate choices about what you want to say, how you want to say it, and what commitments you are willing to make. A tool can suggest phrasing. You remain responsible for the message.

Our example is fictional. Jordan, a job seeker, wants to thank a hiring coordinator after a short information interview. Jordan’s notes say: “Thank Casey for explaining the entry-level support role. Say the conversation helped me understand that the team values clear notes and patient explanations. Ask whether the application portal is still open. I am interested, but I do not want to claim I have experience I do not have.” Jordan asks a conversational AI tool for a warm, professional email under 120 words.

The first draft sounds polished: “Dear Casey, Thank you for your generous time. Our conversation confirmed that I am an ideal fit for your team, and I am confident I can immediately improve customer satisfaction. I have extensive experience documenting complex cases. I have already submitted my application, but I would be grateful if you could keep me in mind for future leadership opportunities.” The message has several problems. It says Jordan is an ideal fit, which Jordan has not established. It claims extensive experience that is not in the notes. It says an application was submitted, though Jordan only wanted to ask whether the portal is open. And it introduces leadership opportunities that were never discussed.

Even the good parts need a voice check. “Generous time” may sound formal in a way Jordan would not choose. The reader needs a sincere thank-you, a specific detail from the conversation, and one clear question. Jordan is not asking the tool to invent a persona or strengthen qualifications. The job is to help organize the real message.

A better follow-up might say: “Keep the note warm and straightforward, like a thoughtful message from an early-career applicant. Use only these facts: Casey explained the entry-level support role; the team values clear notes and patient explanations; Jordan is interested; application status is unknown. Do not claim experience, fit, results, or actions that are not in the notes. Include one question about whether the portal is still open. Give a draft under 120 words.” This instruction protects the underlying intent and provides boundaries for the language.

Jordan should then review every sentence. Does it sound like something Jordan could say aloud? Does it make an accurate claim? Does it preserve the recipient’s name and role? Does it ask the question Jordan intended? A revised version might read: “Hi Casey, Thanks for taking time to explain the entry-level support role. I appreciated hearing that the team values clear notes and patient explanations. I’m interested in applying. Is the application portal still open? Thanks again, Jordan.” Jordan can edit “appreciated” to “liked” if that feels more natural, or add a personal detail from the actual conversation. The final choice belongs to Jordan.

Here is a practical voice check you can use. First, mark the message’s purpose in one sentence. Second, circle any phrase that claims a fact, skill, feeling, or commitment. Ask: “Did I provide evidence for this, and do I stand behind it?” Third, identify the relationship and audience. A note to a teammate may be direct and informal; a public service notice may need plain, neutral wording. Fourth, read the draft aloud or imagine the recipient asking, “What do you mean by this?” If you would need to explain or retract a sentence, revise it before sending.

Now consider transparency. Should Jordan tell Casey, “An AI tool helped me write this”? There is no universal phrase that answers every situation. The right choice depends on the rules and expectations that apply. A workplace may have a policy. A course may require disclosure. A public-facing or high-stakes document may call for explicit review and attribution. A private, low-stakes wording suggestion may not need a special announcement if no rule or expectation requires it. The point is to make a thoughtful decision rather than assume either that every small edit must be disclosed or that AI assistance should always be hidden.

Use four questions. One: Is there a policy, agreement, or assignment instruction that requires disclosure? Follow it. Two: Did AI materially shape the substance, reasoning, or claims, rather than only suggest wording? The more it shaped the content, the more important clear disclosure becomes. Three: Would the audience reasonably expect to know how the work was produced in order to assess trust, authorship, or accountability? Four: Can you accurately describe what the tool did and what you personally checked? If yes, a brief, specific statement is more useful than a vague claim that “AI did everything.”

For example, if Jordan only used a tool to shorten a thank-you note and personally checked every sentence, the situation may differ from one where a tool drafted a portfolio statement about qualifications. In the second case, the content affects how an employer evaluates Jordan, so Jordan should take particular care to verify the claims and follow any disclosure expectations. If Jordan cannot explain or defend a claim, the solution is not merely to disclose that AI wrote it. The unsupported claim should be removed or verified first.

Transparency is also not a substitute for responsibility. Saying “AI generated this” does not make a false statement acceptable. Nor does human review mean the tool played no role. A clear note might say, “I used an AI tool to organize my draft, then checked the dates and edited the wording.” That describes the assistance and the human action. Use such a note only when it is relevant or required, and keep it accurate.

There are ways to preserve voice without asking the tool to imitate someone else. Give it your own key points, audience, purpose, and boundaries. You might provide a short sample of your own neutral writing if it is appropriate and contains no sensitive information, but do not upload private messages or someone else’s personal data without permission. Better still, describe observable qualities: “plain language, one short paragraph, friendly but not overly casual.” Ask for suggestions rather than a finished identity. Then revise the output yourself.

If no AI tool is available, this practice still works. Write a rough note, then use the four-part voice check. Or ask a partner to suggest edits and mark which ones you accept. The important habit is to own the claim, the tone, and the final decision.

Recap: voice is a set of choices about meaning and audience. Use AI suggestions to support those choices, not replace them. Remove unsupported claims and commitments. Decide about disclosure by checking rules, the degree of AI contribution, audience expectations, and your ability to explain the process. Be specific when you disclose. In the next week, we will apply the same ownership principle to factual claims: we will match each claim with evidence and decide whether to accept, revise, verify, or reject it.
