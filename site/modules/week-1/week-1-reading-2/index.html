Deciding When AI Fits

Learning objectives: Explain when AI is useful, when another method is preferable, and how to communicate the role and limits of AI-assisted work.; Identify strengths, limits, uncertainty, and the need to verify generated material.; Choose a suitable low-risk AI use or explain why another method is preferable.

Content

Deciding when AI fits: match the method to the task

Objectives: identify-ai-limits, choose-low-risk-fit Estimated reading time: 7 minutes

Figure: Choose a method based on consequence, uncertainty, and whether the result can be checked.

Availability is not a reason

When conversational AI is easy to open, it can be tempting to start with the tool and search for something to do with it. A better starting point is the work. What needs to be accomplished? What would a useful result look like? What information is necessary? Who could be affected if the result is wrong?

A tool is a method for doing work. Like any method, it has strengths and limits. Generative AI may help with language and pattern-heavy tasks, such as producing a first draft, organizing made-up examples, or suggesting alternative wording. It may be unnecessary for work that is already quick, or unsuitable where the answer depends on current authoritative information, private records, or professional judgment.

The same topic can contain a low-risk and a high-risk task. “Write a friendly headline for a fictional open house” is different from “choose which person should be hired.” Both require language, but the consequences, fairness concerns, evidence needs, and accountability are very different.

Use a fit check

A practical fit check has six parts:

1. Name the work. Write a one-sentence task beginning with an action: draft, summarize, classify, compare, or organize. Avoid broad instructions such as “handle this.” A bounded task is easier to inspect.

2. Identify the input. What material would the system need? Can you use fictional or public information? Remove anything unnecessary. Never put personal, employer-confidential, client, student, or regulated information into an AI service unless the specific use is explicitly approved and protected by the organization's rules. For this course, activities use fictional details only.

3. Consider impact. What could happen if a wrong or biased result were used? A weak brainstorm can be discarded. A wrong eligibility, hiring, safety, or financial recommendation may harm someone. Greater impact calls for stronger safeguards, qualified review, and often a different method altogether.

4. Plan the check. Can the output be compared with a source or clear criteria? A draft based on a short fact sheet can be checked sentence by sentence. An opaque recommendation about a person may be difficult to validate. If you cannot say how you will check the result, do not treat the response as ready to use.

5. Consider reversibility. Can you correct the output before it affects anyone? A private draft is easy to discard. An automatic message, financial transaction, or record change may be difficult to undo. Do not let a generated response trigger consequential action without authorized boundaries and human checkpoints.

6. Keep a named decision-maker. Who accepts or rejects the result? If the answer is “the AI,” stop. A person with the appropriate role must remain responsible for the decision and any communication or action.

The check does not produce a numerical score. It helps you choose among three paths: try a small reviewed step, redesign with stronger limits and qualified oversight, or use a non-AI method.

Compare three fictional tasks

A fictional youth center needs a slogan for a public art day. A worker supplies only the event's public theme and asks for four options. They inspect the options for clarity and misleading promises. This could be a low-risk, reversible trial. The tool contributes ideas, and the worker chooses or writes the final slogan.

A fictional office needs to summarize a public, approved checklist into five action items. The worker can compare the summary against the checklist and confirm all required steps remain. This could also be suitable if the source is safe to share and the worker checks the transformation. If the list concerns safety, the appropriate subject expert still needs to approve the final instructions.

A fictional employer wants a ranking of job applicants from their applications. The input contains personal information, the ranking may affect opportunities, and it may be hard to explain or validate how the model treated each person. The appropriate response is not “paste the files and see.” Follow established hiring policy, use qualified human review, and do not delegate the decision to a general AI tool. Even if a specialized tool is authorized for some administrative task, its permissions and human review must be explicit.

Run a bounded trial

For a task that appears suitable, record a baseline before using AI. How is the work done now? How long does it take? What quality standard does it need to meet? Then test one small task using safe input. Ask for a specific output and review it against the standard. Note errors, corrections, time spent, and whether the result actually helped.

For example, a worker might compare the time needed to draft a fictional event notice directly with the time needed to prompt, review, and correct an AI draft. The AI response may save typing but add fact-checking work. The full task time is the useful comparison, not just the time until the first output appears.

A trial is evidence gathering, not a commitment to adopt. You may discover that the tool performs well only for one narrow step, or not at all. You may need to revise the request, improve the source material, or use another method. Stop if the input cannot be made appropriate or the output cannot be checked to the level the task requires.

Make non-use a deliberate choice

People sometimes describe non-use as falling behind. That framing hides important questions. A method should serve the work and the people affected by it. A calculator is helpful for arithmetic, but an unsupported number still needs a reliable source. A generative tool is helpful for a draft, but it does not become the authority on current policy.

Good professional judgment includes choosing the tool, setting its boundary, and deciding not to use it. It also includes explaining that choice in practical terms: “This task uses private client information, and our approved process does not permit this service,” or “The official policy document is the source, so I will check it directly.”

Text equivalent of the decision path

A plain-text version is: Describe the task → check input safety and impact → decide whether output can be verified and corrected → name the human decision-maker → run a small trial or choose another method. If any step reveals an unresolved risk, pause and redesign rather than treating an AI response as the answer.
