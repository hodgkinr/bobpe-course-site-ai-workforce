Choose a Suitable Task—or Do It Another Way

Learning objectives: Describe in plain language how generative AI produces outputs, distinguish generated responses from verified facts, and identify when information may be incomplete or stale.; Explain when AI is useful, when another method is preferable, and how to communicate the role and limits of AI-assisted work.; Choose a suitable low-risk AI use or explain why another method is preferable.

Content

Lecture 2 summary: Choose a suitable task—or do it another way

This lecture gives learners a repeatable way to decide whether AI fits a work task. The decision looks at task characteristics, input safety, consequence, checkability, and reversibility. Through fictional examples, learners contrast a low-risk first draft with a sensitive staff decision and a current-policy question. The lecture reinforces that “AI optional” is a real choice and that a small, reviewable trial is safer than handing over an entire outcome.

Objectives: identify-ai-limits, choose-low-risk-fit Estimated viewing time: 10 minutes (script is approximately 1,280 spoken words at 125 words per minute)

Lecture script

Lecture 2 video script: Choose a suitable task—or do it another way

Estimated speaking time: about 10 minutes at 125 words per minute. Objectives: identify-ai-limits, choose-low-risk-fit.

[OPENING]

A manager at a fictional neighborhood arts center asks two staff members for help. One task is to draft three welcoming headlines for a public open-house flyer. The other is to decide which applicant should receive a job offer. Both involve words. That does not make them equally suitable for AI.

Today we will use a practical decision process to choose where AI may help, where it needs stronger limits, and when another method is better. The goal is not to use AI more often. The goal is to make a sound choice for the task and the people affected by it.

[SECTION 1: DESCRIBE THE TASK]

Start by stating the work in a sentence that begins with a verb: “draft three headline options,” “summarize these public event notes,” or “check this fictional inventory list for duplicate item names.” A clear task statement makes it easier to see what the tool might do and what it must not do.

Ask whether the task is mainly about language, patterns, or organizing material. If so, generation may provide a useful first pass. Then ask what information the tool would need. Could you use a fictional example, public text, or a brief summary rather than real personal or confidential information? If useful performance requires uploading sensitive records, that is a reason to stop and find another way or follow an approved process. Never use a personal account to enter employer, client, student, or regulated information.

Next, look at the consequence of an error. A weak headline can be rewritten before it is printed. A wrong eligibility decision may affect someone's livelihood. A missing step in a safety instruction could put people at risk. The greater the consequence, the less appropriate it is to rely on generated output without qualified review and established procedures. For some decisions, AI should not be involved at all.

[SECTION 2: CHECKABILITY AND REVERSIBILITY]

Two useful questions are “Can I check it?” and “Can I undo it?” A draft based on a short approved fact sheet is easy to check: compare each sentence to the facts. A summary of invented meeting notes can be checked against the original notes. A list of ideas can be inspected one by one.

Some tasks are harder to check. A generated legal interpretation may sound convincing while missing an important exception. A ranking may hide why it preferred one person. A recommendation based on incomplete information cannot be made reliable just by asking the model to be more confident. When a result is difficult to verify, the task needs a trusted expert, official source, or a different method.

Reversibility matters too. Drafting a message in a document is easy to undo. Automatically sending it to hundreds of customers is not. A generated suggestion that a person can review before use is different from an automated action that changes records, grants access, spends money, or communicates on someone's behalf. Keep the tool's role bounded to a step that a person can inspect.

[SECTION 3: APPLY A FIT TEST]

Here is a quick fit test. Describe the task. Identify the input and remove anything unnecessary or sensitive. Estimate the impact of an error. Decide how you will verify the result. Identify the person who will make the final decision. Consider whether the action can be reversed. Then choose one of three paths: try a small AI-assisted step with review; redesign the task with tighter boundaries and qualified oversight; or use a non-AI method.

Return to the arts center examples. For the open-house flyer, the staff member can provide public facts—the date, time, and event description—and ask for several headline ideas. The employee checks that none adds a false promise, chooses a suitable option, and edits it for the center's voice. This is a bounded, reversible, low-risk use.

For selecting a job applicant, the task has a significant effect on a person. A model might reproduce bias, overlook context, or give an unexplained ranking. The organization may also have rules about what tools can be used. The appropriate path is not to submit applications to a generic chatbot and accept its ranking. Use the organization's approved hiring process and qualified human judgment. If a permitted tool supports an administrative step, its role and checks must be explicitly authorized and designed so that it does not make the decision.

What about asking for the center's current refund policy? A model may not have the current policy. The safer step is to consult the official policy document or the responsible staff member. If AI helps rephrase a policy passage, compare the wording directly against the official text and do not treat the model as the source.

A fourth example is a schedule change. A worker has an approved fictional schedule and wants a clear reminder for a practice team. A model might make the notice shorter, but a missing time zone or an omitted group could send people to the wrong place at the wrong time. A checkable task would name the facts that must stay intact, ask for a draft only, and require comparison with the approved schedule before sharing. The possible consequence is modest compared with hiring, but it is still worth preventing avoidable confusion.

Notice how the fit test changes based on the question, not the novelty of the tool. If the same schedule reminder includes a real employee's accommodation or personal contact details, the input decision changes. If the system can send the reminder automatically to a large mailing list, reversibility changes. A task that looked suitable as a private draft may need to be redesigned when the information or action changes. Revisit the fit check any time the task expands.

You can also make the choice visible to coworkers. A short explanation might say, “I used a tool to suggest wording from the public event facts. I checked the date and entrance against the approved plan, then edited the final message.” This is more informative than saying only “AI wrote it.” It tells the reader what role the tool played and what human review occurred. Follow any applicable disclosure policy when the work will be shared.

[SECTION 4: USE A SMALL TRIAL]

When a task passes the fit test, begin with a small trial. Choose one piece of work, write down the expected quality, and keep a copy of the source material. Ask for a bounded output. Review it against specific criteria. Record what you corrected and whether it saved time or improved the result. If it did not help, stop or revise the approach.

A trial is not a promise to adopt a tool. It is a way to learn whether a proposed use is worth keeping. A simple baseline matters: how long does the task take without AI, and what quality standard must the result meet? If the AI-assisted version adds checking work or makes the output worse, that is useful evidence against using it.

[RECAP AND TRANSITION]

To choose a suitable task, consider the nature of the work, input safety, possible harm, checkability, reversibility, and who holds the decision. Start with an optional, bounded step. Keep consequential decisions with accountable people and use the approved method. A good decision can be “not here, not for this task.”

In the next week, we will focus on the part you control before a model responds: defining the task, giving it relevant context safely, and asking for an output shape you can use. A well-framed request can improve a suitable use, but it cannot turn an unsuitable task into a safe one.
