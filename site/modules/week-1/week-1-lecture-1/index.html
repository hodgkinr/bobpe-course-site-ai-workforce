What Generative AI Does

Learning objectives: Describe in plain language how generative AI produces outputs, distinguish generated responses from verified facts, and identify when information may be incomplete or stale.; Explain a practical, plain-language model of how generative AI produces content from instructions and context.

Content

Lecture 1 summary: What generative AI does

This lecture introduces a practical mental model: a generative AI system uses patterns learned from examples and the current conversation to produce a likely continuation or transformation. It can be useful with language and pattern-heavy work, but a fluent response is not evidence that a claim is true. Learners distinguish generation from verification, recognize the role of task framing, and keep the person responsible for the result. A fictional scheduling example shows how AI can draft options while a human checks constraints and decides what to send.

Objectives: explain-generative-ai, identify-ai-limits Estimated viewing time: 10 minutes (script is approximately 1,250 spoken words at 125 words per minute)

Lecture script

Lecture 1 video script: What generative AI does

Estimated speaking time: about 10 minutes at 125 words per minute. Objectives: explain-generative-ai, identify-ai-limits.

[OPENING]

Imagine you have been asked to write a short notice telling a fictional team that a practice meeting has moved from Tuesday to Wednesday. You know the new time, the audience, and the tone. A conversational AI service can suggest a clear draft in seconds. That can be useful. But the service could also invent a room number, change the time, or sound more certain than the information you gave it. The important skill is not being impressed by the draft. It is knowing what the tool did and what you still need to do.

In this lecture, we will build a plain-language model of generative AI, look at what makes it helpful, and name the limits that should shape your decisions.

[SECTION 1: GENERATING A RESPONSE]

Generative AI is software that produces new material, such as text, images, audio, or code, in response to instructions and context. In a text conversation, a large language model uses learned patterns in language to produce a likely sequence of words. The prompt and conversation provide context for that generation. The system is not retrieving a guaranteed answer from a perfect book of facts. It is generating a response that fits patterns and the request as it has been framed.

A useful comparison is autocomplete expanded into a flexible writing and pattern tool. Ordinary autocomplete may suggest the next word. A language model can continue a paragraph, reorganize notes, or produce a first draft because it has learned many patterns in how language is used. The comparison has limits: the model can handle complex instructions, but that does not mean it has the same experiences, goals, or judgment that a person has.

This model explains both the benefit and the risk. Pattern generation can make a draft sound natural and organized. It can also produce a natural-sounding detail that was never provided or checked. NIST describes this risk as confabulation: a generated answer can present incorrect content confidently. For our everyday use, remember the short version: a fluent response is a candidate, not proof.

[SECTION 2: WHERE IT CAN HELP]

Generative AI often helps with tasks where a person can define the goal, provide suitable material, and review the result. Examples include brainstorming several subject lines, turning fictional notes into a draft checklist, changing the reading level of a public announcement, or sorting sample comments into themes for a human to inspect.

These tasks share a few features. The work is language-heavy or pattern-heavy. A first pass is useful even if it needs editing. The input is safe to share. The result is easy to check before anyone relies on it. If the tool makes an error, the person can catch it and undo the change.

Consider our fictional team notice. The person can ask for a friendly, two-sentence draft using only three facts: the practice meeting is now Wednesday, it begins at 2 p.m., and the location remains the training room. The person can then compare the response with those three facts. If the draft says 3 p.m. or invents a different room, reject or revise it. The human can send the final notice because the human owns the schedule and knows who needs to receive it.

AI is optional. If the notice takes less time to write directly than to prompt, inspect, and correct a model, write it yourself. If the job requires careful personal judgment or an authoritative answer, use a qualified person or trusted source. Good use includes choosing not to use AI.

[SECTION 3: WHAT IT DOES NOT GUARANTEE]

A model may produce a wrong detail, miss a constraint, misunderstand an ambiguous request, or reflect unfair patterns found in examples it learned from. It may be out of date. It may also format a response beautifully while quietly leaving out something important. These are not all the same problem, so the check should fit the task.

For a simple transformation, compare the output to the supplied material: did every date, name, amount, and condition remain correct? For a factual claim, look for a reliable source that supports the claim. For advice involving safety, money, employment, health, legal rights, or another significant consequence, do not rely on a generated answer as the authority. Follow the relevant policy and consult an appropriate qualified person.

The system's confidence is not a dependable signal of correctness. Phrases such as “certainly,” “the answer is,” or even a tidy source list do not prove that the content is true. A model can generate a plausible citation that does not support its claim. Check evidence directly when evidence matters.

A second example helps show why the distinction matters. Suppose a fictional warehouse team has a public opening-hours notice that must be translated into a plain-language summary. The model may help shorten the wording, but it could drop an exception such as “closed on the first Monday of each month.” The worker should compare the summary with the full notice, paying special attention to dates, exceptions, names, and numbers. If the source is the authority, the source remains the authority after the rewrite.

It is useful to separate what the model contributes from what the person contributes. The model may propose wording, structure, or a pattern worth considering. The person supplies the goal, selects which information is appropriate, recognizes what matters in the workplace, checks the response, and accepts responsibility for the next action. These are different contributions. Giving the system a clear task does not transfer the person's responsibility for checking whether the result is fit for use.

A response can also be incomplete without being obviously wrong. If you ask for a summary of a policy excerpt and the answer contains only correct statements, it may still leave out the exception that changes what a worker should do. That is why verification includes checking for omissions as well as errors. Make a short list of required details before you review, then confirm each one appears in the output and still means the same thing.

[SECTION 4: A SIMPLE DECISION]

Before asking AI for help, name the goal and ask four questions.

First: Is the task a good match for generating or organizing language and patterns? Second: Can I provide safe, relevant input? Third: Can I check the result against material or criteria I trust? Fourth: Is the decision still mine, with no hidden high-impact action being delegated?

If you can answer yes, a small trial may be reasonable. If one answer is no, redesign the task, choose a safer method, or do not use AI. Start with one bounded step. For example, ask for three possible headings, not for an entire report that will be published without review.

This is a way to preserve agency. You decide whether the tool is appropriate, what information to enter, which parts of the response to use, and what leaves your hands. You can stop at any point.

[RECAP AND TRANSITION]

Today’s mental model is that generative AI creates a plausible response from learned patterns and the current context. This can make it a useful assistant for language and pattern-heavy work. It does not guarantee truth, completeness, current information, or fairness. Your checks and your decision remain essential.

Next, you will compare workplace tasks and decide where AI is a useful low-risk option, where another method is better, and what makes the difference. Keep the rule in mind: Know the input, own the output.
