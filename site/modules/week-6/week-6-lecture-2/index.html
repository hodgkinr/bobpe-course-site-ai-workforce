Test the Workflow and Decide What Comes Next

Learning objectives: Check claims and transformations against appropriate evidence, detect uncertainty or bias, and decide whether to accept, revise, verify further, or reject an output.; Design a bounded professional workflow with appropriate AI or tool use, permissions, checkpoints, escalation paths, stop conditions, and evaluation.; Explain when AI is useful, when another method is preferable, and how to communicate the role and limits of AI-assisted work.; Evaluate a proposed workflow against a baseline and recommend adoption, revision, or non-use.

Content

Lecture 2: Test the Workflow and Decide What Comes Next

This lecture shows how to evaluate a proposed workflow with small, structured tests against a baseline. Learners define quality, time, accessibility, error, and handoff measures before testing; compare AI-assisted results with current practice using at least two fictional cases; and record evidence rather than relying on fluency or novelty. It distinguishes adoption, revision, and non-use decisions and explains how to document limitations, failures, and human responsibility. The lecture closes the course arc by integrating task framing, safe inputs, iteration, verification, oversight, and communication into a transferable workflow dossier.

Objectives: evaluate-ai-workflow; design-ai-workflow.

Lecture script

Video Script: Test the Workflow and Decide What Comes Next

Spoken script (approximately 10 minutes at 130 words per minute):

You have designed a bounded workflow. Now comes the question that is easy to skip: does it help? A fluent sample output or an impressive demonstration is not enough. The workflow must be compared with a reasonable baseline, checked against relevant evidence, and evaluated for both benefits and costs. The goal is not to prove that AI works. The goal is to make a responsible decision about this task.

Let’s return to the fictional community organization inbox. The baseline is the current process: a coordinator reads each message, checks the public information sheet, drafts a reply, and sends it. We want to compare that process with an AI-assisted process that categorizes messages and prepares drafts, while a person checks and sends every reply. Before testing, we need to say what “better” means.

Choose a few measures connected to the purpose. Time is one measure, but it is not the only one. Quality might mean that the category is correct, the reply answers the question, factual details match the current source, and the tone is respectful. Accessibility might mean that the message uses clear language and gives a usable path to help. Safety might mean that sensitive or unusual messages are routed correctly and are never sent without review. Handoff quality might mean a staff member can understand why a case was escalated.

Set the measures before you see the results. Otherwise, it is tempting to choose whichever measure makes the tool look good. For example, a faster draft that contains an incorrect event date is not a success. A correct draft that takes as long to review as the original process may still be useful for consistency, but the evidence should show that. A workflow can provide value in more than one way, yet you should be explicit about which value matters.

Use a small, safe test. This course does not need live customer records. Create fictional cases that represent ordinary requests and edge cases. For example: a simple question about opening hours; a question that combines two topics; an unclear request; a message with a fictional urgent concern; a request outside the approved public information; and a message that should be escalated. Use at least two cases for a first comparison, and more when your decision depends on a pattern. Never make broad claims from a tiny sample.

For each case, run the baseline process and the proposed workflow. Keep the task and reference information the same. Record what happened. Did the AI choose the right category? Did the draft use a correct fact? How much editing was needed? Did a human catch the error? Was the stop rule triggered when it should be? How long did the whole process take, including review and correction? Counting only the AI generation time hides the work people still perform.

Evidence should be inspectable. Keep the fictional case, the source used, the output, the reviewer’s corrections, and the time estimate. If a claim in the draft depends on a source, record which source was checked. Mark uncertainty rather than converting it into a confident statement. A simple table can show case, baseline result, AI-assisted result, issue found, review action, and outcome. That table is more useful than a collection of polished examples because it exposes both success and failure.

Include accessibility and fairness in the evaluation. Could a person understand the message? Does the categorization treat comparable cases consistently? Does the process route a person to a human when their request does not fit a category? Are people with different needs able to use the response and request another format? A short test cannot answer every fairness question, but it can reveal obvious gaps and indicate where more review is needed. If a workflow affects access to a service, involve appropriate people and use the organization’s formal review process.

Now make a decision. Adopt only when the task is suitable, the tool is approved, the checks work, and the evidence shows useful value within acceptable limits. Adoption still needs an owner, documentation, and periodic review. Revise when the workflow may help but the test reveals fixable problems, such as unclear categories, stale source material, or an ineffective stop rule. Revise and test again before expanding use. Avoid or discontinue when the task is not appropriate, the data cannot be handled safely, the tool cannot meet quality needs, the review burden outweighs the benefit, or the consequences are too serious for the available controls.

Do not treat “more automation” as the reward for passing a small test. A successful draft assistant does not automatically justify allowing the system to send messages. Every increase in access or autonomy changes the risk and should be evaluated separately. Keep the system at the lowest authority that supports the purpose. A result that saves a small amount of time while preserving careful human review may be a better design than a faster system with unclear accountability.

Consider how to interpret mixed results. Suppose the assistant sorts four of five routine questions correctly, but one message that should have gone to a person is labeled as routine. A single percentage can make this appear close to success, but the kind of error matters. Mislabeling a harmless hours question may have a different consequence from missing a safety concern or private case. Record which errors occurred, what the workflow did next, and whether a person caught them before harm. Set a quality bar that reflects the stakes, and do not average a serious failure away with several easy successes.

It is also useful to separate a design defect from a tool defect. Perhaps the categories were too vague, the public source was stale, the prompt omitted an important constraint, or the service could not access the source. The evidence may show more than one cause. Write down what changed between the baseline and test, then decide whether the next action is to update the process, correct the source, change the permitted tool, or stop the AI step. Avoid attributing every result to “the model” when the workflow around it may be responsible.

Document the limits. Perhaps the workflow was tested only on routine fictional questions, not on real messages. Perhaps it requires a coordinator to verify every factual claim. Perhaps a particular category often triggers escalation. These are not embarrassing footnotes; they tell the next user where the evidence ends. A responsible handoff describes what the system does, what it cannot establish, what a person must check, and where to report a problem.

Let’s connect the six-week course. We began by understanding that generated responses are plausible outputs, not verified facts. We learned to define a task and provide useful context, iterate on drafts, and verify claims. We then protected information, mapped agent actions, and matched oversight to consequences. In this final week, those skills come together in a workflow dossier and evaluation. The course outcome is not dependence on a product. It is a repeatable way to choose, design, test, and explain AI use.

The final question is a practical one: if someone else takes over this process tomorrow, can they understand the goal, the safe inputs, the permitted tool boundary, the evidence checks, the stop rules, and the human owner? If yes, your design is becoming transferable. If not, make the missing part explicit before recommending adoption.

To recap: define success in advance, compare with a baseline, test routine and edge cases safely, count review and correction time, check quality and accessibility, record limits, and recommend adopt, revise, or avoid. Evidence helps people decide; it does not remove their responsibility. That is how we know the input and own the output.
