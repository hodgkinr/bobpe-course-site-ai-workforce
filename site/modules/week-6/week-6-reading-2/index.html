Evaluate, Document, and Hand Off an AI Workflow

Learning objectives: Check claims and transformations against appropriate evidence, detect uncertainty or bias, and decide whether to accept, revise, verify further, or reject an output.; Design a bounded professional workflow with appropriate AI or tool use, permissions, checkpoints, escalation paths, stop conditions, and evaluation.; Evaluate a proposed workflow against a baseline and recommend adoption, revision, or non-use.

Content

Evaluate, Document, and Hand Off an AI Workflow

Objectives: evaluate-ai-workflow; design-ai-workflow.

Figure: Evaluate the full process against a baseline before deciding what to do next.

Evidence before enthusiasm

A workflow can sound useful and still create extra work or new errors. Evaluation asks whether the process meets a real need with acceptable quality, time, accessibility, and risk. Define success before testing so the measures do not shift to fit the result. A polished response is not proof of accuracy, and one successful example is not proof that the workflow is reliable.

Imagine a fictional public library considering an AI assistant to sort common questions and draft responses from its public hours and event calendar. The current baseline is that a staff member reads each question, checks the calendar, drafts a reply, and sends it. The proposed process adds a model-generated category and draft, but a staff member still checks facts and sends. The evaluation should compare the full processes, including the person’s review time.

Pick measures tied to the task

Measures should reflect what matters to the people who rely on the result. Possible measures include:

  • Quality: Is the question categorized correctly? Does the response address it? Are dates and locations accurate against the source?
  • Time: How long does the entire task take, including setup, review, correction, and escalation?
  • Accessibility: Is the response understandable and does it provide a clear next step or alternate path?
  • Safety: Are sensitive, urgent, or unusual cases routed to a human? Are unsupported claims removed?
  • Consistency: Are comparable cases handled using the same criteria?
  • Handoff: Can the next staff member understand what was checked and what remains uncertain?

Do not assume all measures carry equal weight. A small time saving may not compensate for a serious risk. Write down which failures are unacceptable. For a public schedule response, an incorrect date that sends someone to a closed building may outweigh a few minutes saved. For a task where every draft requires extensive correction, the AI step may have no practical value.

Test with cases that reveal boundaries

Use safe, fictional cases. Include ordinary cases and edge cases: a clear hours question; two questions in one message; a missing date; an outdated source; a request outside the public information; a sensitive concern; and a tool failure. Compare the current process and AI-supported process using the same task and source. If testing with fewer than several cases, describe the work as an early check, not a broad evaluation.

Record each case, baseline result, AI-assisted result, evidence consulted, errors, human corrections, escalation outcome, and total time. Count review time. If a draft takes twenty seconds to generate but four minutes to verify and repair, the total is not twenty seconds. Mark estimates as estimates. Separate observations from interpretations: “three of five drafts used the correct calendar date” is an observation; “the workflow is reliable” is a broad conclusion that needs more evidence.

Review both the normal path and the stop path. Does an unclear message reach a person? Does the workflow stop when its source is missing? Is a generated statement checked against a source? Can a reviewer tell what the system did? A good test includes failure because a workflow that only works when every input is clean is not ready for routine use.

Make a responsible recommendation

There are three useful recommendations. Adopt when the task is appropriate, data handling is approved, results meet the defined quality bar, review is effective, and evidence shows a worthwhile benefit. Adoption still needs a named owner and future review. Revise when issues appear fixable: clarify categories, improve the source, change the prompt or boundary, or add a better checkpoint. Then test again. Avoid or stop when the task is unsuitable, information cannot be handled safely, errors are too serious, controls fail, or the review burden erases the benefit.

A decision should match the evidence. If a small fictional test shows that drafts often cite unsupported dates, the fair recommendation is not to deploy broadly. It may be “revise: limit drafts to questions answered by the current calendar and require source comparison; test again.” If the system cannot reliably distinguish urgent cases, keep those cases outside the AI workflow. A narrow useful role is better than an overbroad claim.

Write a handoff another person can use

The handoff should explain the purpose, who the workflow serves, its inputs and exclusions, the steps, tool permissions, evidence checks, stop rules, owner, and current limitations. Include a small test record with examples. Say what the AI did and what the person verified. If AI assisted a deliverable, communicate that role when policy, audience expectations, or the nature of the work makes disclosure appropriate. Do not imply that a generated claim has been independently confirmed when it has not.

Document maintenance too. Who updates the source? Who receives an error report? When will the workflow be reviewed? Which changes require a new test? A change in audience, data, permissions, or action authority can change the risk even when the prompt remains the same. A human owner should be able to suspend the workflow when a serious problem appears.

When sharing results, be precise about what the test covered. “In four fictional routine cases, the draft matched the public calendar after review” is more useful than “the assistant is accurate.” The first statement names the sample, task, source, and role of human review. It also leaves room to test more cases. This kind of calibrated language helps a colleague understand the evidence without mistaking a limited pilot for a guarantee.
