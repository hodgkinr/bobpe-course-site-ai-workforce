Design a Workflow Around a Real Need

Learning objectives: Design a bounded professional workflow with appropriate AI or tool use, permissions, checkpoints, escalation paths, stop conditions, and evaluation.; Design a bounded, repeatable AI-supported workplace workflow with clear inputs, checks, and handoffs.

Content

Lecture 1: Design a Workflow Around a Real Need

This lecture turns a vague wish to “use AI” into a bounded process. Learners select a repeatable, low-risk task; document its current baseline; map the people, inputs, steps, decisions, and handoffs; and identify which step, if any, could benefit from AI. It shows how to specify a tool boundary, safe context, output format, verification points, failure paths, stop conditions, and responsible handoff. A fictional inbox-triage example illustrates that a workflow may use AI for categorization or draft preparation while leaving sending, sensitive cases, and final decisions with people. Learners leave with a first workflow map for the final project.

Objectives: design-ai-workflow; protect-sensitive-information; set-oversight.

Lecture script

Video Script: Design a Workflow Around a Real Need

Spoken script (approximately 10 minutes at 130 words per minute):

When people hear “AI workflow,” they may picture a complicated machine with many connected services. A useful workflow can be much simpler. It is a repeatable process with a clear purpose, defined inputs, steps, checks, and a responsible handoff. The important question is not where AI can be inserted. It is whether a particular step can be improved while keeping the process safe and accountable.

Let’s use a fictional example: a small community organization receives routine questions about opening hours, event registration, and accessibility. A staff member spends time sorting the messages and drafting replies. Some messages involve private information, urgent needs, complaints, or unusual requests. The organization is considering an AI-supported workflow. We will design it in a way that makes boundaries visible.

Start with the task, not the tool. “Use AI to handle the inbox” is too broad. It does not define what counts as handling, whose messages are included, or what happens when a message is sensitive. A better task statement is: “Help the coordinator sort fictional or approved routine messages into three categories and prepare a draft response using the current public information sheet. A staff member reviews every category and draft before anything is sent.” This task has a goal and a human owner.

Next, describe the baseline. What happens today? Who reads the messages? How many steps are involved? How long does the sorting and drafting take? What errors or delays matter? If you do not know, gather a small sample using safe, approved methods or build a fictional simulation. You need a baseline so you can later decide whether the new workflow actually helps. Without one, “it feels faster” is not enough to justify more risk.

Now map the people and handoffs. The requester sends a message. The coordinator checks it. Routine questions may be answered from an approved public information sheet. Sensitive, urgent, or unusual cases go to a staff member with the right role. The final reply is sent through the organization’s normal channel. Affected people include the person who wrote in, the coordinator, and anyone whose access or service might be affected by the response. Name these groups because their needs shape the design.

Then inventory the inputs. The AI step should receive only what it needs. For training, use invented messages. In a real process, use only an approved service and the minimum appropriate information. Do not send a complete mailbox or unrelated history when the task only needs one message. If messages may contain personal or regulated information, define a rule for detecting and routing them without exposing more information than necessary. If the organization cannot safely provide the input, that step is not suitable for AI.

Choose one bounded AI contribution. Perhaps the tool can label a message as “routine hours,” “registration,” or “needs human review,” and prepare a draft for the first two categories. It should not decide who deserves a service, determine whether a complaint is valid, or send a reply on its own. The tool boundary says what it may read and create, and what it may not do. Read-only access to a public information sheet is different from access to private records. Drafting is different from sending.

Write a simple step sequence. First, a person confirms that the message belongs in the pilot and contains no out-of-scope issue. Second, the AI receives the approved text and reference sheet. Third, it suggests a category and, if appropriate, a draft. Fourth, the coordinator checks the category, compares any factual claim with the source, and edits the response for tone and accessibility. Fifth, a person sends it using the usual channel. If the system identifies uncertainty, sensitive content, urgency, or a topic outside the categories, it stops and routes the message to an authorized staff member.

This sequence includes checkpoints, but each check needs a clear object. The coordinator compares hours and registration details with the current public sheet. They confirm the category matches the actual question. They check that the response is understandable and does not promise something the organization cannot provide. If the response involves accommodation or a personal case, the coordinator follows the established human process instead of relying on a generated reply.

A good workflow also considers failure. What if the reference sheet is missing? Stop drafting and ask for the current source. What if the message includes several topics? Label it for human review. What if the AI produces a confident but unsupported date? Remove the claim and verify from the official schedule. What if the tool is unavailable? Use the normal manual process. What if a category is uncertain? Do not force the message into the nearest available label. Escalate.

That example shows why the workflow map should describe categories as well as steps. “Routine” should have an observable meaning. For instance, it could mean a message asks one question answered directly by the current public information sheet and contains no sensitive, urgent, or individual case. The exception list should be equally understandable. If operators disagree about which category applies, the workflow needs a clearer rule or a human-first path. Avoid categories that sound simple but hide subjective judgments such as “important,” “difficult,” or “good candidate.”

When selecting the AI contribution, compare it with a non-AI option. A saved template, improved search page, clearer public schedule, or shared checklist may solve the same problem with fewer new risks. Perhaps the actual delay comes from an outdated schedule rather than the time it takes to draft. Fixing the source could improve both manual and AI-supported work. A useful design records why this step may benefit from AI and what evidence would change that conclusion. AI is one possible method among several, and choosing another method can be a successful result of the design process.

Accessibility should shape the workflow from the beginning. The intended user may need a plain-language response, a screen-reader-friendly structure, or a way to speak with a person. The workflow should not assume that every person can follow a link, fill out a digital form, or interpret a dense paragraph. Include these needs in the task and review checklist. If the tool produces a draft that is technically accurate but hard to use, the coordinator should revise it before sending. If the request needs specialized support, route it rather than improvising an answer.

Now consider accessibility. A fast draft is not useful if it is hard to read, excludes people who need another format, or assumes everyone can use the same channel. Ask whether the response uses plain language, whether information is organized clearly, and whether a human can offer an alternate way to get help. Include affected users in evaluation where appropriate. Do not assume that a model’s polished language is automatically accessible.

Finally, document ownership. Who maintains the reference sheet? Who can review messages? Who updates the categories? Who is contacted if the tool behaves unexpectedly? A workflow without an owner can quietly drift: sources become stale, staff assume someone else is checking, or a draft begins to be sent automatically. Set a review date and conditions that would suspend the pilot.

Let’s recap the design sequence: define a specific task, record the baseline, identify people and handoffs, minimize inputs, choose one bounded AI role, map steps, add evidence-based checkpoints, define failure and stop rules, and assign an owner. The human decision is visible at the point where a draft becomes a real communication.

In the next lecture, we will evaluate a workflow with evidence. A workflow earns its place only if it improves a meaningful outcome without creating unacceptable costs or shifting risk to people who were not part of the design.
