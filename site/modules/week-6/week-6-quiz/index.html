Week 6 Applied Judgment Quiz

Learning objectives: Check claims and transformations against appropriate evidence, detect uncertainty or bias, and decide whether to accept, revise, verify further, or reject an output.; Design a bounded professional workflow with appropriate AI or tool use, permissions, checkpoints, escalation paths, stop conditions, and evaluation.; Explain when AI is useful, when another method is preferable, and how to communicate the role and limits of AI-assisted work.; Design a bounded, repeatable AI-supported workplace workflow with clear inputs, checks, and handoffs.; Evaluate a proposed workflow against a baseline and recommend adoption, revision, or non-use.

Week 6: Workflow Design and Evaluation

Apply baseline, boundary, verification, and evaluation judgment to a workplace workflow.

Question 1. A manager says, ‘Let’s use AI to handle customer messages.’ What is the best first move when designing a workflow?
The task needs a specific purpose, scope, output, and human role before a tool is selected. A narrow definition makes inputs and boundaries possible to evaluate.
Question 2. A team says its new AI-assisted process is ‘much faster’ after trying one message. What should it do before recommending adoption?
A meaningful evaluation defines measures in advance and compares the full proposed process with a baseline across safe cases. One message is only an early example, not strong evidence of broad performance.
Question 3. A workflow drafts replies from a public schedule. Which test set best checks whether it has a safe boundary?
A useful test includes routine cases and cases that trigger uncertainty or escalation, such as missing sources and out-of-scope requests.
Question 4. An AI draft takes 15 seconds to produce, but a coordinator spends four minutes correcting dates and tone. Which measure best represents the task’s time cost?
Evaluation should count total process time, including generation, review, correction, and escalation. Generation time alone hides work shifted to the person.
Question 5. A coordinator wants to test a message-sorting workflow but has not confirmed that live messages may be shared with the service. What should the first test use?
Fictional cases can test the workflow design without sharing real personal or confidential messages. Live data should only be used under an approved process.
Question 6. A generated reply includes an event date. Which checkpoint is most appropriate before sending it?
A reviewer should compare the date to the current authoritative schedule and remove or correct unsupported details before sending.
Question 7. A small fictional test shows that the workflow handles routine questions well but often invents answers when the schedule is unavailable. What is the most responsible recommendation?
The test reveals a fixable but important stop-rule failure. Revise the workflow so it stops when the source is unavailable, then retest before adoption.
Question 8. A two-case paper simulation found that draft preparation saved an estimated three minutes per case, but reviewers corrected one unsupported claim. What should the recommendation say?
A small test supports only a limited conclusion. Report the estimate and correction, require the relevant check, and recommend further testing or a narrow revision rather than broad adoption claims.
Question 9. A draft workflow makes replies faster, but some messages use jargon and omit how a person can request another format. What evaluation or design change is most relevant?
Accessibility is part of usefulness. Define clarity and alternate-path checks, then have a reviewer revise the draft before delivery and include these criteria in future evaluation.
Question 10. A colleague will take over a workflow pilot next month. Which handoff best supports safe continuity?
A responsible handoff explains purpose, permitted inputs and actions, checks, stop/escalation rules, evidence and limitations, and who owns review and maintenance.
