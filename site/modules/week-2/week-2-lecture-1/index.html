Turn a Vague Request into a Task Brief

Learning objectives: Turn a workplace need into a clear request with goal, audience, context, constraints, and a useful output format; then improve the request through iteration.; Convert a vague workplace request into a clear task brief.

Content

Lecture 1 summary: Turn a vague request into a task brief

This lecture teaches a practical task brief with five parts: goal, audience, context, constraints, and output shape. Learners diagnose vague requests, add only the information needed for a useful response, and define how they will judge the result. A fictional community-center notice shows how a request can become more actionable without requiring a long or elaborate prompt. The lecture frames prompt writing as task communication rather than magic wording.

Objectives: convert-vague-request, specify-output-format Estimated viewing time: 10 minutes (script is approximately 1,250 spoken words at 125 words per minute)

Lecture script

Lecture 1 video script: Turn a vague request into a task brief

Estimated speaking time: about 10 minutes at 125 words per minute. Objectives: convert-vague-request, specify-output-format.

[OPENING]

Suppose a coworker sends you the message, “Can you make this better?” It is a request, but it leaves many questions open. Better for whom? Better in what way? What should stay the same? What should the finished result look like?

People often send similarly vague requests to AI and then blame themselves when the answer misses the mark. A better approach is to treat the prompt as a small task brief. In this lecture, we will build a brief from five useful parts: goal, audience, context, constraints, and output shape.

[SECTION 1: GOAL]

Start with the goal: what should the response help you accomplish? Use a concrete verb. “Draft,” “summarize,” “compare,” “organize,” and “rewrite” describe different work. “Help with this” does not.

For example, “Draft a short reminder for the fictional community center’s open house” names the action and the subject. You can make the goal still clearer: “Draft a friendly reminder that tells visitors the open house starts at 6 p.m. on Thursday.” A precise goal gives the system a direction. It does not ensure the result will be accurate, so the source facts still need to be checked.

[SECTION 2: AUDIENCE]

Next, say who will read or use the result. A message for first-time visitors may need a welcoming explanation. A note for staff may need a direct action and a deadline. Audience information helps with word choice, amount of detail, and tone.

Do not assume the system knows your audience just because the topic seems familiar. “For people who have never attended our open house” is more useful than “make it good.” Audience is about the needs of the reader, not a license to stereotype. Avoid unsupported assumptions about a group. If a specific audience need matters, state the practical need: “Use short sentences and explain the entrance location.”

[SECTION 3: CONTEXT]

Context is the relevant background the system needs. It can include source text, purpose, known facts, or what has already been decided. It should be enough to do the task and no more. Use fictional, public, or approved information. Do not paste names, contact details, private work records, client messages, student information, or regulated information into an AI service.

A task brief could say: “The fictional center is hosting a free open house on Thursday at 6 p.m. Visitors can tour the art rooms. The west entrance is open.” Those are the relevant facts. If the center's real visitor list is not needed to create a reminder, keep it out of the prompt. Safe context means choosing and minimizing information, not merely adding a warning sentence.

[SECTION 4: CONSTRAINTS]

Constraints set boundaries. They can include length, tone, reading level, details to include or avoid, and facts that must not change. For the reminder, constraints might be: “Keep it under 80 words. Do not add a registration requirement. Preserve the stated time and entrance.”

Constraints work best when they can be checked. “Make it friendly” is somewhat subjective. “Use a warm, welcoming tone; avoid exclamation marks; include one clear next step” is easier to inspect. A constraint does not replace your review. If the response invents a requirement, remove it even if the text otherwise sounds polished.

[SECTION 5: OUTPUT SHAPE]

Tell the system what form would be useful. Would you like one paragraph, a table with specified columns, three bullet points, or a draft followed by questions? Output shape can make review easier. If you need to compare several choices, a table may help. If a person must read the result aloud, a short script may be more useful than a dense paragraph.

Do not request a format that hides uncertainty or implies unsupported authority. For example, asking for a “final approved policy” does not make the model an approver. Ask for “a draft summary of the fictional policy text below” and compare the output with that text.

[SECTION 6: PUT THE BRIEF TOGETHER]

Here is a complete example:

“Draft a reminder for first-time visitors to the fictional community center’s open house. The open house is free, starts Thursday at 6 p.m., and visitors may tour the art rooms through the west entrance. Keep the message under 80 words and welcoming. Do not add a registration requirement or change any facts. Return one short paragraph and a checklist of facts you used.”

The goal is to draft a reminder. The audience is first-time visitors. The context contains the three relevant facts. The constraints set length, tone, and factual boundaries. The output shape is one paragraph plus a checklist. The request also gives us a way to evaluate the result: compare the paragraph with the three facts and remove any unsupported detail.

Notice that this is not a magic formula. It is ordinary clear communication. You can use fewer details when the task is simple, or add more constraints when they help. A long prompt is not automatically a good prompt. Include the information that changes what a useful answer should be.

[SECTION 7: REVIEW THE BRIEF BEFORE YOU SEND IT]

Before using a brief, read it as if you were the person who has to check the output later. Can you tell what the task is? Can you identify the intended reader? Are the supplied facts enough to complete the job? Did you include a constraint that could be counted or checked? Does the requested format make comparison easy? If you cannot answer one of those questions, adjust the brief before sending it.

Here is a quick repair. A vague request says, “Tell people about the new room.” A better brief might be: “Draft a short announcement for fictional center members. The new art room opens on Monday, and the public tour begins at 4 p.m. Keep the announcement under 70 words, do not invent a room number, and return one paragraph plus a list of facts used.” The worker then checks the date, tour time, and absence of an invented room number. The brief does not need decorative language. It needs enough detail to make a usable draft and a reliable review possible.

Another repair is to separate the task from the decision. “Choose the best person for this assignment” asks the model to make a judgment that could affect people. “Organize these fictional, approved task requirements into a checklist for a supervisor to review” describes a bounded transformation. In both cases the words “best” or “choose” deserve attention. They may signal that a prompt is asking the tool to decide something that should remain with a person.

Finally, treat a prompt as a starting point for communication, not a one-time formula. If the first response reveals that the system misunderstood the audience, you can add that audience need. If it invents a fact, restate the source boundary and review the revision. If it continues to miss the requirement, stop and use another method. Clear instructions improve the chance of a useful response; they cannot guarantee correctness or safety.

[RECAP AND TRANSITION]

A task brief names the goal, audience, relevant context, constraints, and output shape. It helps a conversational AI produce a response that is easier for you to inspect. Keep the input safe, avoid unnecessary details, and define how you will review the result.

Next, we will practice deciding which context belongs in a prompt and how to describe a usable format. The person framing the task remains responsible for the request and the final work.
