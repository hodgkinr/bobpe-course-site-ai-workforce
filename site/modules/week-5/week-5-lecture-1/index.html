Protect Information Before You Prompt

Learning objectives: Choose what information is appropriate to share, account for privacy, fairness, accessibility, and policy, and retain human accountability.; Apply data-minimization and approval checks before sharing information with an AI service.

Content

Lecture 1: Protect Information Before You Prompt

This lecture helps learners make deliberate choices about information before using an AI service. It distinguishes public, fictional, internal, personal, confidential, and regulated information; shows how to replace details with fictional stand-ins; and emphasizes that removing a name alone may not make a story safe. Learners use purpose, minimum necessary detail, policy, and consequence to decide whether to share, transform, or keep information out of a service. It connects privacy to fairness: incomplete or skewed inputs can produce harmful recommendations even when no secret is exposed. The recurring decision is: what does the task need, what can safely be supplied, and who remains accountable?

Objectives: protect-sensitive-information; set-oversight.

Lecture script

Video Script: Protect Information Before You Prompt

Spoken script (approximately 10 minutes at 130 words per minute):

Imagine you are asked to write a friendly reminder for a customer who has not returned a borrowed item. You have the customer's name, phone number, account history, and the item due date. A conversational AI service might help with tone and structure. The useful question is not simply, “Can the tool write this?” It is, “What information does the writing task actually require, and what am I allowed to share?”

This week we are moving from individual drafts to connected workplace actions. Before we discuss tools and agents, we need a habit that applies to every use of AI: decide what information belongs in the input. Our course principle is “Know the input, own the output.” The person who supplies information is responsible for choosing it thoughtfully, and the person who delivers the result is responsible for checking it.

Let’s begin with a few categories. Public information is intended for anyone to see, such as a published store address. Fictional information is invented for practice. Internal information is meant for a defined workplace audience, such as a draft schedule. Personal information can identify or describe a person, such as a name, email, account number, or details about their health or finances. Confidential information is restricted because disclosure could harm a person or organization. Regulated information is subject to specific legal or professional rules. These categories can overlap. A customer record is both personal and likely confidential; a public document can still contain personal details.

You do not need to memorize a universal classification chart. Organizations have different policies and tools. Instead, use a decision routine. First, state the task. Second, identify the minimum information needed to complete it. Third, identify the information’s source and sensitivity. Fourth, check the applicable policy and the service’s approved use. Fifth, choose a safer path if anything is unclear. This can mean using an approved service, asking a supervisor or privacy contact, using synthetic information, or doing the task without AI.

Return to the reminder example. A writing assistant needs the purpose, tone, and perhaps the fact that an item is overdue. It does not need the customer’s name, phone number, payment history, or full account record to draft a template. We can replace the real details with placeholders: “[customer name]” and “[return date].” We might ask for a short, respectful reminder that does not imply a penalty or make claims about policy. Then a person who is authorized to handle the account can fill in details using the approved system and verify the facts.

That is data minimization: provide only the information needed for the task. It reduces exposure and often makes the request clearer. But be careful: removing a name is not always enough. A combination of details can identify someone. “The only night-shift nurse at the Northside clinic who reported an incident last Tuesday” might point to a person even without a name. When details are not essential, generalize them. Use “a staff member at a clinic” or create a clearly fictional scenario. For practice activities in this course, fictional information is the default.

What if the work itself depends on sensitive details? Do not assume that changing names makes it safe. Follow the organization’s rules, use only approved systems, and ask an appropriate human when you are unsure. Some tasks should not be entered into a general-purpose service at all. If a tool is approved for a particular purpose, that approval still has limits: the tool may be approved for drafting but not for making decisions about people, or approved with certain data settings but not others. Approval is a boundary, not a substitute for judgment.

Privacy also connects to fairness. Suppose an AI tool is asked to summarize applications for a training program. If the input contains gaps, biased descriptions, or inconsistent criteria, a polished summary can make those problems appear neutral. A model generates a response from the supplied context; it does not guarantee that the context is fair or complete. Before a people-affecting decision, ask whether the criteria are relevant, whether every person was evaluated consistently, and whether a qualified human is responsible for the decision. Do not ask a model to infer character, reliability, or potential from irrelevant personal details.

Now let’s make the learner’s decisions visible. For our reminder task, the goal is a respectful reusable message. The safe input is the general situation, requested tone, and constraints. The output is a draft template. Evidence comes from the approved account record and current policy, not from the generated draft. The human decision is whether the message is accurate, appropriate, and authorized before it is sent. The AI can help with wording; it cannot establish that the customer owes anything or that a fee applies.

Use this same frame for any task: goal, input, output, evidence, human decision. Ask yourself: What is the goal? What input is truly needed? What output will the tool produce? What evidence must I consult? What decision must remain with a person? If you cannot answer these, pause and clarify the task.

Here are practical signs to pause: the task includes information about a person; the service’s approval is uncertain; the output could affect access to a job, housing, education, money, safety, or a service; the request asks for an inference about someone; or the result will trigger an external action. These signs do not automatically mean “never use AI.” They mean the care level should rise. A person may need to remove details, use a different method, add review, or decline the AI step.

One more example shows how this routine works when the task is not just writing. Imagine a team wants to summarize feedback forms after a fictional training session. The forms include comments about the session and a few details about individual participants. The goal may be to identify common themes, not to produce a profile of each participant. Before any tool is used, the team should ask whether the feedback is approved for that service, whether names or distinctive details are needed, and whether an aggregate summary can answer the question. If comments mention a personal issue, that does not mean the comment should be copied into an AI prompt. A human can separate operational feedback from a concern that needs a private, appropriate response.

If you are the learner completing a workplace task, you may not have authority to decide whether a service is approved or to transform restricted data. That is not a reason to guess. Write down the task in general terms, identify the category of information involved without copying it into a new service, and ask the person responsible for the data or policy. You can still make progress by drafting a blank template, creating a fictional example, or mapping a manual process while you wait for guidance. Asking the right person is part of completing the work responsibly.

Let’s recap. Classify information broadly, but use your local policy for the actual rule. Minimize what you provide. Remember that combinations of details can identify someone. Prefer fictional or generalized examples in practice. Check whether a service is approved for the information and task. And treat fairness as part of input quality, not an afterthought.

Next, we will examine what it means for an AI system to use tools or act in a loop. The same privacy habits will matter there, but we will add another question: what is the system allowed to do, and where must a person review or stop it?
