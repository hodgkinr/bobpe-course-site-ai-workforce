Agents, Tools, and Human Control

Learning objectives: Describe in plain language how generative AI produces outputs, distinguish generated responses from verified facts, and identify when information may be incomplete or stale.; Design a bounded professional workflow with appropriate AI or tool use, permissions, checkpoints, escalation paths, stop conditions, and evaluation.; Explain how an agent workflow can combine a model, instructions, tools, observations, and orchestration.; Match permissions, checkpoints, stop rules, and escalation to impact and reversibility.

Content

Lecture 2: Agents, Tools, and Human Control

This lecture introduces agent workflows in plain language. A model generates candidate steps or content; instructions shape its role; tools let a system retrieve information or make changes; a harness manages the run; and orchestration coordinates multiple steps or services. The lecture follows a fictional supply-request task through a bounded loop and distinguishes suggestion, preparation, and execution. Learners match autonomy to impact, reversibility, and uncertainty, then define checkpoints, permissions, stop rules, and escalation. It stresses that tool access is not proof of correctness and that humans must own consequential decisions.

Objectives: explain-agent-workflows; set-oversight.

Lecture script

Video Script: Agents, Tools, and Human Control

Spoken script (approximately 10 minutes at 130 words per minute):

Imagine a workplace assistant that receives a request for office supplies, checks a catalog, compares the request with a spending limit, and prepares a purchase for approval. A basic chat assistant might only suggest what to order. A more connected system could retrieve catalog information or create a draft order. If it can submit the purchase itself, its actions can have a real cost. The difference is not just a smarter answer. It is a change in what the system can do.

Today we will build a plain-language map of agent workflows and a practical way to choose oversight. An agent is a system that uses a model in a repeated process to pursue a goal. It may plan a step, use a tool, inspect the result, and then decide what to do next. A tool is a defined capability, such as searching an approved catalog, reading a document, or creating a draft. The model proposes or selects actions; the tool performs the operation it was built to perform.

Several related terms help us describe the arrangement. Instructions tell the system its task and boundaries. A harness is the surrounding software that manages a run: it can provide context, limit tool access, record steps, or require approval. Orchestration coordinates steps or multiple services, such as sending a request through a search step, a classification step, and a draft step. These terms are used differently by different people and products. The durable idea is to ask what the system can access, what it can change, who controls the sequence, and where a person can intervene.

Let’s walk through the supply request. The goal is to prepare a proposed order for a team’s shared printer supplies. The input should include an approved fictional request, the relevant catalog, and a spending rule. It should not include unrelated employee records. The model may interpret the request and propose a catalog item. A search tool may retrieve the listed price and availability. The workflow could compare the total with a threshold, prepare a draft, and stop for a human to review it.

Notice that every step has a boundary. The search tool is read-only. It searches one approved catalog. The draft tool can create an unsubmitted order. It cannot pay or submit. The spending rule is provided from a current source. A person confirms that the item and quantity are appropriate. If the catalog is unavailable, the system stops. If the price is missing or the request is ambiguous, it asks for clarification rather than guessing. That is a bounded workflow: a purpose with explicit inputs, permissions, review points, and a stop condition.

We need a simple way to decide how much autonomy to allow. Consider three factors: impact, reversibility, and uncertainty. Impact asks how much harm or cost could result from a wrong action. Reversibility asks how easily an action can be undone. Uncertainty asks how likely the system is to have incomplete or ambiguous information. A low-impact, reversible formatting suggestion with clear input may need a quick human review. A decision that spends money, changes a record, or affects a person’s opportunity needs stronger control. High uncertainty should also narrow what the system can do.

You can think of autonomy as a ladder. At the first rung, the AI only suggests. At the next, it prepares a draft for a human. Higher up, it may perform a reversible action after approval. At the most consequential rung, it acts without a person approving each step. There is no reason to climb higher simply because a tool offers that setting. Choose the lowest level that still helps with the task. As impact rises, review should become more explicit and authority should be more limited.

What does a good checkpoint look like? It names what the human will inspect and what evidence will support that inspection. “Review the order” is vague. “Compare item, quantity, price, and delivery date with the approved request and current catalog before submission” is actionable. A checkpoint should occur before an irreversible step, before an external message is sent, or before a decision affects a person. A human should be able to understand the proposed action without having to trust a hidden chain of generated reasoning.

Stop rules make the boundary clear. The workflow stops if it encounters a missing or contradictory fact, an unsupported item, a cost above the limit, an unavailable source, a tool error, a request outside the allowed scope, or a signal that the action may affect someone in a way the workflow was not designed to handle. Escalation tells the system or user what happens next: ask the requester, send the case to an authorized person, or switch to a manual process. A useful stop rule does not ask the system to “use its best judgment” about a high-impact exception.

Let’s make the five-part frame visible again. Goal: prepare a proposed supply order. Input: a fictional request, catalog, and rule. Output: a draft, not a purchase. Evidence: current catalog data and the approved request. Human decision: confirm the item, quantity, cost, and authorization. Tool access is part of the design, but it does not supply truth by itself. The person still checks whether the retrieved source is relevant and current.

Some systems can operate through several services. For example, orchestration might retrieve a document, summarize it, and prepare an email. A mistake in an early step can travel downstream. This is why checkpoints should be placed at meaningful transitions: after retrieval, before an external action, and when uncertainty appears. A workflow log can help a reviewer see what information was used and what tools were called. A log supports review, but it does not guarantee the steps were correct.

Before you use any connected system, ask: What is its goal? Which information can it access? Which tools can it call? Can it read, draft, edit, send, buy, or delete? Which actions are reversible? Who approves? What causes it to stop? Who handles exceptions? If the answers are unclear, do not give the system broader access in order to “see what happens.” Narrow the task and clarify the boundary first.

It also helps to distinguish a checkpoint from an audit after the fact. Suppose an assistant drafts an order and a person sees only the total. The person may approve it without noticing that the system selected the wrong size or delivery location. A checkpoint should expose the details that connect the action to the request. The person should be able to see the source item, quantity, cost, and any assumptions. If the system cannot present that information clearly, the person cannot provide meaningful approval. The workflow should be redesigned to make review possible, or the action should stay manual.

To recap: agents combine a model with instructions, tools, and a repeated process. Harnesses and orchestration shape how that process runs. More access means more possible consequences. Match autonomy to impact, reversibility, and uncertainty. Make checkpoints observable and specific. Define stop and escalation rules before the workflow runs.

In Week 6, we will use these ideas to design a complete workflow and test whether it helps. We will compare it with a baseline, examine quality and accessibility, and decide whether to adopt, revise, or avoid it. A well-designed workflow is valuable because it serves a real need with clear limits, not because it contains the most automation.
